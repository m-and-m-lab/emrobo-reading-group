Reading ahead is optional. The presenter covers the paper, so come even if you haven't opened it. With fifteen minutes, skim the abstract, the main figure and the limitations.

Questions worth bringing to any session:

  • What does the model actually know about the robot's body, and where does that knowledge enter?
  • What would change on a Spot, a dexterous hand, or your own platform?
  • Which assumptions hold only for the robots in the experiments?
  • What happens when the robot drifts from its nominal model: a heavier payload, a weak actuator, a noisy sensor?

Present a paper

One main paper or a small set of closely related papers. Keep the talk focused enough to leave 15–20 minutes for discussion, 60–90 minutes in total.

The presenter template

  1. Frame the paper
  2. What is the problem setting, and what are the assumptions?
  3. What is the core idea, in one sentence?
  4. The representation
  5. What representation of embodiment or skill is used?
  6. How is that representation integrated into the method?
  7. What is new compared with prior work?
  8. Our reading
  9. How should we understand the method from our own perspective?
  10. What downstream capability does the representation enable?
  11. The evidence
  12. What is the evaluation setup: metrics, environments and embodiments?
  13. Two strengths and two weaknesses.
  14. Reproducibility: code, data, simulator and hardware requirements.
  15. Your angle
  16. One extension relevant to your lab's work or your own interests.
  17. Discussion, around 15–20 minutes.

Timeline

  1. 2+ weeks beforeClaim a slot and confirm the paper with the organizers.
  2. 1 week beforeSend the paper link and any paired reading for the announcement.
  3. Day ofBring two or three open questions to start the discussion.
  4. Within 3 daysShare your slides, and say whether you're happy for the recording to be published.

Tips

  • Lead with a figure of the representation: the graph, the tokens, the chain or the embedding. It's worth more than the equations.
  • Place the paper on a timeline: what it builds on and what came after.
  • Be generous with the weaknesses. They usually start the best part of the discussion.

Run a Tooling Hour

Monthly, practical and informal, 1–2 hours.

Share an open-source tool, codebase, simulator, hardware setup or workflow that others can actually use, together with the lessons, tricks, limitations and setup issues you ran into. Demo it live if you can. Several short demos can share one session.

Recording and sharing

Sessions are hybrid on Zoom. Over time we'd like to build a public archive of discussions and practical resources on embodiment representations and embodied skill learning.

  • With the consent of presenters and participants, selected sessions may be recorded and uploaded to the group's YouTube channel.
  • Everyone is reminded at the start of a recorded session. If you'd rather not appear, keep your camera off and ask in the chat; questions are read aloud without your name.
  • Presenters can review their recording before it's published and ask for it to be removed at any time. Unpublished work is never recorded unless the presenter asks for it.
  • Upcoming sessions, recordings, papers and tooling demos may be shared on LinkedIn and in relevant robotics communities.
