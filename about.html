The idea

We want to understand how representations of the robot's body appear, or fail to appear, in current robot learning work on physical action and interaction: what gets represented, how that representation is used or adapted, and what capabilities emerge when embodiment is modeled as first-class structure rather than absorbed implicitly through data, heuristics or modular handoffs.

Recent works such as Body Transformer and GET-Zero encode robot morphology and connectivity directly in the architecture. In parallel, planning work such as the Virtual Kinematic Chain and sequential mobile manipulation planning unifies base, arm and scene kinematics, and shows where splitting mobile manipulation into separate locomotion and manipulation modules can lose feasibility or robustness.

The community is moving from robot-specific policies toward cross-embodiment learning. We'd like to push that conversation beyond transfer itself, toward how the choice of embodiment representation affects downstream reasoning, planning, control and interaction. A related direction is embodied skill learning and skill representation, particularly for mobile and dexterous manipulation: how skills can be represented, transferred or adapted across different bodies, instead of staying tied to one platform or one low-level action space.

What we mean by embodiment

Broadly, the physical properties and constraints that shape how a robot can act and interact with the world.

In general
Morphology, kinematics, dynamics, contacts, sensing, actuation limits, controller interfaces, latency, stability, and other hardware-dependent constraints.
In mobile manipulation
Base–arm kinematics and nonholonomy, reachability and manipulability, sensor placement and occlusion, contact modes, and whole-body stability.
Off nominal
Actuator degradation, payload changes, sensor noise, damage, and changing contact conditions. Adapting to these depends on some meaningful representation of what the robot can currently do.

Why this area

1. Understanding embodiment is a first step toward aligning different robot models

Many methods succeed on a specific platform, sensor layout or controller interface, but transfer poorly because embodiment is not explicitly represented, even when the task looks similar. Embodiment-structured methods such as Body Transformer and GET-Zero target this directly by exposing robot structure to the learning system. A better understanding of these representations may clarify what information should be shared across platforms, what must stay robot-specific, and what can be adapted online.

2. Task reasoning depends on the robot doing the task

A task that is straightforward for one robot may need a different base placement, viewpoint, grasp, contact strategy or motion sequence for another, because of differences in reachability, sensing, stability or actuation. VKC- and SMMP-style formulations show how explicitly representing these coupled constraints improves feasibility and exposes actions that are missed when navigation and manipulation are treated separately. We want to know whether learning-based systems can get similar benefits from better embodiment representations.

3. Embodiment matters even more when the robot has to adapt

Real robots don't stay identical to their nominal model: actuators degrade, payloads change, sensors get noisy, contacts vary and hardware can partially fail. Should an embodiment model represent only the nominal structure, or also the robot's changing effective capabilities through learned or inferred representations? This connects to the internal–external framing in our previous embodiment work: a robot needs some representation of its own physical capabilities to reason about physical interaction with the world.

4. Skill learning adds another level of representation

A skill represented directly in joint space may be highly robot-specific. A very abstract task representation may transfer more easily, but may lose the physical constraints needed for feasible execution. We're interested in shared or transferable skill representations, including UniSkill-style approaches. Body representation describes what the robot can physically do; skill representation describes reusable structure in how tasks are performed.

5. What makes a useful embodiment representation is still unclear

The field uses kinematic graphs, morphology tokens, geometry, inertial parameters, latent embeddings, sensorimotor history, force and contact information, robot IDs, task-space abstractions, and combinations of these. It isn't obvious which suits which problem, or what is lost when moving from explicit physical models to learned abstractions. Comparing them across areas of robotics is the point of the group.

What we look for

We don't expect any method to have all of these. Part of the point is to see which capabilities current representations support well and where the gaps are.

  • Generalization across embodiments with minimal robot-specific tuning
  • Adaptation to changes in the robot's physical capabilities
  • Support for contact-rich manipulation and locomotion
  • Support for long-horizon and whole-body tasks
  • Transferable or reusable skill representations
  • Clear sim-to-real transfer
  • Interpretable links between the body representation and behavior
  • Evaluations that separate robot, sensor, morphology and dynamics changes
  • Downstream improvements attributable to the representation itself
