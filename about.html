The idea

We want to understand how representations of the robot's body appear, or fail to appear, in current robot learning work on physical action and interaction.

In particular: what gets represented, how that representation is used or adapted, and what capabilities emerge when embodiment is modeled as first-class structure rather than absorbed implicitly through data, heuristics or modular handoffs.

The community is moving from robot-specific policies toward cross-embodiment learning. We'd like to push that conversation beyond transfer itself, toward how the choice of embodiment representation affects downstream reasoning, planning, control and interaction.

What we mean by embodiment

Broadly, the physical properties and constraints that shape how a robot can act and interact with the world.

In general
  • Morphology
  • Kinematics
  • Dynamics
  • Contacts
  • Sensing
  • Actuation limits
  • Controller interfaces
  • Latency
  • Stability
  • Other hardware-dependent constraints
In mobile manipulation
  • Base–arm kinematics and nonholonomy
  • Reachability and manipulability
  • Sensor placement and occlusion
  • Contact modes
  • Whole-body stability
Off nominal
  • Actuator degradation
  • Payload changes
  • Sensor noise
  • Damage
  • Changing contact conditions

What we're asking

Recent works such as Body Transformer and GET-Zero show how architectural choices can encode robot morphology and connectivity directly, for better learning and control. In parallel, planning work such as the Virtual Kinematic Chain (VKC) and sequential mobile manipulation planning (SMMP) gives explicit representations that unify base, arm and scene or object kinematics. Those formulations show where splitting mobile manipulation into separate locomotion and manipulation modules can lose feasibility or robustness.

This matters most in mobile manipulation, where locomotion, reaching, sensing, grasping, contact and stability are tightly coupled. It matters again when the robot deviates from its nominal condition. Adaptation to actuator degradation, payload changes, sensor noise, damage or changing contact depends on having some meaningful representation of what the robot can currently do.

A related direction is embodied skill learning and skill representation, particularly for mobile and dexterous manipulation. As robot learning moves toward reusable, transferable skills, the way a skill is represented becomes tied to the body executing it. We're interested in how skills can be represented, transferred or adapted across different bodies and physical constraints, instead of staying coupled to one platform or one low-level action space.

Why this area

Five reasons from the proposal. Sessions tend to come back to one or more of them.

  1. Understanding embodiment is a first step toward aligning different robot models

    Many methods succeed on a specific robot platform, sensor layout or controller interface, but transfer poorly because embodiment is not explicitly represented, even when the task itself looks similar.

    Embodiment-structured methods such as Body Transformer and GET-Zero target this problem directly by exposing robot structure to the learning system. For mobile manipulation this is particularly relevant, because whole-body behavior depends on the interaction between base motion, manipulation, sensing and contact.

    A better understanding of embodiment representations may help clarify what information needs to be shared across robot platforms, what needs to remain robot-specific, and what can be adapted online.

  2. Task reasoning depends on the capabilities of the robot executing the task

    Different embodiments may need different strategies for the same task. A task that is straightforward for one robot may need a different base placement, viewpoint, grasp, contact strategy or motion sequence for another, because of differences in reachability, sensing, stability or actuation.

    In mobile manipulation, success often depends on coordinated base positioning, viewpoint selection and manipulation under clutter, occlusion, limited reachability or embodiment failures. VKC- and SMMP-style formulations show how explicitly representing these coupled constraints can improve feasibility and expose actions that may be missed when navigation and manipulation are treated separately.

    We want to understand whether, and how, learning-based systems can get similar benefits from better embodiment representations.

  3. Embodiment matters even more when the robot has to adapt

    Real robots do not stay identical to their nominal model. Actuators degrade, payloads change, sensors become noisy, physical contacts vary and hardware can partially fail. These changes directly affect what the robot can do.

    That creates a representation problem: should an embodiment model represent only the nominal robot structure, or also capture the robot's changing effective capabilities through learned or inferred representations?

    The motivation is closely related to the internal–external framing in our previous embodiment work: a robot needs some representation of its own physical capabilities to reason effectively about physical interaction with the external world.

  4. Skill learning introduces another level of representation

    For reusable robot skills, there is also the question of what should transfer across embodiments. A skill represented directly in joint space may be highly robot-specific. A very abstract task representation may transfer more easily, but may lose the physical constraints required for feasible execution.

    We're interested in shared or transferable skill representations, including UniSkill-style approaches, and how they interact with embodiment for mobile and dexterous manipulation. Body representation describes what the robot can physically do; skill representation describes reusable structure in how tasks are performed.

  5. What makes an embodiment representation useful is still unclear

    The field uses many representations: kinematic graphs, morphology tokens, geometry, inertial parameters, latent embeddings, sensorimotor history, force and contact information, robot IDs, task-space abstractions, and combinations of these.

    It is not obvious which representation suits which problem, or what is lost when moving from explicit physical models to learned abstractions. The reading group gives us a way to compare these approaches across different areas of robotics and build a better understanding of which representation choices actually matter.

What we look for

Desirable properties we'd like to understand through the literature. We don't expect any method to satisfy all of them; part of the point is to see which capabilities current representations support well and where the gaps remain.

  • Generalization across embodiments with minimal robot-specific tuning
  • Adaptation to changes in the robot's physical capabilities
  • Support for contact-rich manipulation and locomotion
  • Support for long-horizon and whole-body tasks
  • Transferable or reusable skill representations
  • Clear sim-to-real transfer
  • Interpretable links between the body representation and the resulting behavior
  • Evaluations that separate robot, sensor, morphology and dynamics changes
  • Downstream improvements that can be attributed to the representation itself

Who it's for

Robotics PhD and MS students and research staff interested in mobile manipulation, learning, planning, whole-body control, embodied perception, dexterous manipulation, sensing and benchmarking.

For now we're starting with a smaller test run of three or four labs actively working in related areas, and will expand once the structure of the group is settled. Sessions are hybrid, in FRB and on Zoom, and selected sessions may be recorded with consent to build a public archive.
